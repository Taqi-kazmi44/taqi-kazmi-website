UN Experts Demand Immediate Release of Lawyers Imaan Mazari-Hazir and Hadi Ali Chattha

Four UN experts in Geneva called on Pakistan on 30 September 2026 to immediately release human rights lawyers Imaan Mazari-Hazir and Hadi Ali Chattha and drop unfounded criminal and terrorism charges against them, warning that invoking an older case right after bail appears to use the criminal justice system as punishment for freedom of expression and human rights advocacy.

What was reported

  1. Four UN experts in Geneva issued a press release on 30 September 2026 calling on Pakistan to immediately release human rights lawyers Imaan Mazari-Hazir and Hadi Ali Chattha and drop unfounded criminal and terrorism charges against them.
  2. The couple were rearrested on 17 September 2026 on terrorism-related charges linked to a separate older case, hours after the Supreme Court granted them bail. The accusation dates to March 2025 and concerns participation in a protest authorities allege was illegal.
  3. The experts had previously expressed alarm at their conviction for social media posts supporting local activists and criticising the military.
  4. After the re-arrest, an Islamabad anti-terrorism court refused a police request for 30-day custody and interrogation, and sent them to jail on 14-day judicial remand instead. On 22 September 2026, an anti-terrorism court returned their bail petitions because they lacked signed powers of attorney.
  5. The four experts are Margaret Satterthwaite (independence of judges and lawyers), Ben Saul (counter-terrorism and human rights), Andrea Bolanos (human rights defenders) and Gina Romero (freedom of peaceful assembly and association).

The key legal point

  1. The experts' core concern is the timing: "Invoking an older case immediately after they were granted bail appears to be an attempt to use the criminal justice system as punishment for freedom of expression and human rights advocacy."
  2. Bail is a judicial finding that the person need not remain in custody. Rearresting them within hours on a different, older charge raises the question the experts are now asking publicly: was the second arrest grounded in fresh legal necessity, or was it a reaction to the court's bail order?
  3. The anti-terrorism court's own decisions show the judiciary exercising independent review: it rejected 30-day police custody in favour of judicial remand, and returned bail petitions for a procedural defect (unsigned powers of attorney) rather than deciding them on the merits.
  4. Why it matters: when bail is followed by immediate rearrest, public confidence in the courts' protective role takes a hit. Whether such rearrest is lawful depends on whether the new charges stand on their own evidence, not on whether the court was right to grant bail.

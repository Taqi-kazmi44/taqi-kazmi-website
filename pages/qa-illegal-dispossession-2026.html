Can Someone Forcibly Take Over Your Property in Pakistan?

A very common question: if someone breaks into your house, plot or shop and occupies it by force, what does the law say? The short answer is no. The Illegal Dispossession Act, 2005 was made exactly for this situation.

The rule: Section 3 of the Illegal Dispossession Act, 2005

Section 3(1) says no one may enter upon any property to dispossess, grab, control or occupy it without lawful authority, with the intention of dispossessing the owner or occupier.

The punishment is serious

Under Section 3(2), a person who does this can be imprisoned for up to ten years, with a fine, and the victim must also be compensated in accordance with Section 544-A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898. A forcible dispossession that falls outside Section 3(1) carries up to three years imprisonment or a fine, or both, under Section 3(3), added by the Illegal Dispossession (Amendment) Act, 2017.

The remedy is fast: complaint to the Court of Session

You do not start with an FIR. Under Section 4, the complaint goes directly to the Court of Session, and the offence is non-cognizable, which means the police cannot start the case on their own. The court may direct the police to investigate and report within 15 days, and the trial must proceed day to day and be decided within 60 days (Section 5).

Interim relief: get the property back before the trial ends

As interim relief, the court can order the property back to you even before the trial ends, if the occupier is prima facie not in lawful possession (Section 7, Eviction and mode of recovery as an interim relief).

One important limit

The Lahore High Court has held that this Act is meant for organised or calculated grabbing by property grabbers, not for ordinary disputes between co-owners, co-sharers, landlords and tenants, or where the matter is already pending before a civil or revenue court. Those disputes take the ordinary civil route. (Lahore High Court full bench, Writ Petition No. 11952 of 2006.)

If someone forcibly occupies your property

  1. Keep your ownership documents safe and do not take the law into your own hands.
  2. Take the complaint to the Court of Session promptly through a lawyer.
  3. Ask the court for interim relief under Section 7 so the property can be restored to you while the trial is pending.
  4. Remember the limit: if the dispute is really a co-ownership, tenancy or civil matter, the ordinary civil route applies.

The key legal point

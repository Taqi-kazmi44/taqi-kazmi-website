Can You File an FIR for Fraud in Pakistan?

One of the most common questions ordinary people ask: someone promised a plot, a job, a visa or a business deal, took the money, and disappeared. Can the police register an FIR for that? The answer is yes, if the case is really cheating and not only a broken promise. The Pakistan Penal Code, 1860 has a clear answer in Sections 415 and 420.

What Section 415 of the Pakistan Penal Code says: cheating

Section 415 defines cheating. Whoever, by deceiving any person, fraudulently or dishonestly induces the person so deceived to deliver any property to any person, or to consent that any person shall retain any property, or intentionally induces the person so deceived to do or omit to do anything which he would not do or omit if he were not so deceived, and which act or omission causes or is likely to cause damage or harm to that person in body, mind, reputation or property, is said to cheat. The Lahore High Court set out this definition and the text of Section 420 in Ch. Muhammad Arshad v S.H.O. Police Station FIA/SIU, 2006 PLJ Lahore 158.

In plain words: cheating needs deception first, and then the victim, because of that deception, hands over property or does something he would not otherwise do.

Section 420: cheating that leads to delivery of property

Section 420 is titled "Cheating and dishonestly inducing delivery of property." It says: whoever cheats and thereby dishonestly induces the person deceived to deliver any property to any person, or to make, alter or destroy the whole or any part of a valuable security, or anything which is signed or sealed, and which is capable of being converted into a valuable security, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine.

So the punishment for this kind of fraud is imprisonment up to seven years, and a fine as well. Section 417 covers simple cheating, where no property changes hands, with imprisonment up to one year, or fine, or both.

Can the police register an FIR? Yes, Section 420 is cognizable

Under the Second Schedule of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898, an offence under Section 420 is cognizable, which means the police can register an FIR under Section 154 CrPC and investigate without an order from a magistrate. The offence is also non-bailable. Take the proof with you to the police station: payment receipts, bank transfer records, messages, and any written promise or agreement.

Fraud or only a civil dispute? The dishonest intention test

The key point is dishonest intention from the start. If the person made a false promise or hid the truth at the time of taking the money, with no intention of keeping the promise, that is cheating. But if the person honestly intended to keep the promise and later failed, for example a business failed or a loan could not be repaid, that is usually a civil dispute about a broken promise, not a crime. Courts look at the intention at the time of the promise or inducement, not only at the fact that money was lost.

What this means for you

If someone took your money by deceiving you, keep every proof, payment receipts, messages and any written promise, and report the fraud at the police station with those documents. An FIR under Section 420 PPC can be registered because the offence is cognizable. But be ready for the police to ask whether there was deception from the start, because a simple failure to repay, without deception, is a civil matter.

The key legal point

  1. Yes, an FIR can be filed for fraud: cheating is defined in Section 415 PPC, and when it dishonestly induces delivery of property, Section 420 applies, punishable with imprisonment up to seven years and a fine.
  2. What this means for you: keep the proof, payment receipts, messages and any written promise, and report the fraud at the police station with those documents.

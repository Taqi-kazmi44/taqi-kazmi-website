Supreme Court: Open Eyes of the Deceased Do Not Prove a Death Was Unwitnessed

In a nine-page judgment, the Supreme Court of Pakistan ruled that the open or closed state of a deceased person's eyes cannot conclusively establish that the incident was not witnessed, rejecting a common defence argument in criminal trials.

On Tuesday, a three-member bench headed by Justice Muhammad Hashim Khan Kakar dismissed jail petitions filed by Azmat Ullah alias Motu and Muhammad Luqman alias Gona. The petitioners had challenged their convictions in a 2011 double-murder case, which the Lahore High Court had upheld on May 18, 2022. The bench upheld the convictions under Section 302(b) of the Pakistan Penal Code but maintained the High Court's decision to commute the death sentences to life imprisonment.

The defence had argued that the post-mortem and inquest reports recorded the eyes of both victims as open, which indicated that the murders had not been witnessed, because close relatives would ordinarily close the eyes of a deceased person after death.

In the nine-page judgment authored by Justice Salahuddin Panhwar, the court rejected this reasoning. The Qanun-i-Shahadat Order, 1984, does not impose a legal duty to close a deceased person's eyes; the practice is customary and religious, not legal. While Article 129 of the Order allows courts to presume facts they consider likely in the circumstances of a case, human behaviour cannot be assessed in a vacuum or through rigid assumptions: responses to trauma, terror and violent loss vary from person to person.

The judgment added a forensic dimension. The position of the eyelids is governed by physiological processes rather than the presence or absence of witnesses. After death, muscles initially relax, allowing eyelids to remain open or partially open, a condition known as lagophthalmos. Handling or moving a body may also cause previously closed eyes to reopen before rigor mortis develops. Relatives and bystanders may also avoid touching a body to prevent contamination of evidence before forensic experts arrive.

Concluding that neither forensic literature nor the law recognises closed eyes as an indicator of a witnessed death, the court held that open eyelids alone cannot establish that a crime was unwitnessed. The judgment further observed that minor discrepancies and natural variations in eyewitness accounts, arising from the passage of time and the rigours of cross-examination, do not undermine otherwise credible and consistent testimony.

What this means for you: a case cannot be decided on assumptions about customs alone. Courts look at real evidence and credible testimony, not on what someone "should have done" in a moment of shock.

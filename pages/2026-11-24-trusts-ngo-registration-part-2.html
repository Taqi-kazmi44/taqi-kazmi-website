Trusts and NGO Registration (2/6): The Trust Deed and Registration in Sindh

You have decided to create a trust. What do you actually write, where do you file it, and when does the trust legally exist? The answer is in two documents and one registration, and skipping any of them leaves the trust invalid or non-functional. This part walks through the trust deed and the registration process under the Sindh Trusts Act, 2020, step by step.

The Trust Deed: the Instrument of Trust

Section 2(l) calls the document by which a trust is declared the instrument of the trust. In practice, this is the trust deed. Section 4(1) says a trust is created when the author of the trust indicates with reasonable certainty, by any words or acts: an intention to create a trust, the purpose of the trust, the beneficiary, the trust property, and the transfer of the trust property to the trustee, unless the trust is declared by will or the author is himself to be the trustee. A deed usually records all of this, plus the trust's name and registered office, the trustees and how they can be changed or removed, the trustees' powers and duties, how accounts will be kept, and how the trust can be dissolved. The Sindh Trust Rules, 2020 also require a Memorandum of Association, which defines the relationship of the author with the trustees and states the objectives for which the trust is formed. The deed is written on stamp paper, and stamp duty applies under the Stamp Act, 1899.

Validity: When a Trust Legally Exists

Section 3 sets the validity rule. For immovable property, no trust is valid unless it is declared by a non-testamentary instrument in writing, signed by the author of the trust or the trustee and registered, or by the will of the author or the trustee, and its ownership is transferred to the trust. For movable property, no trust is valid unless it is declared in the same way, or the ownership of the property is transferred to the trust. Two things must therefore happen for land or a building: the deed must be registered, and the ownership must actually be transferred to the trust. A signed but unregistered deed for immovable property creates no valid trust.

Step 1: the Trustee Accepts, in Writing

Section 10 says a trust is accepted by any words or acts of the trustee indicating with reasonable certainty such acceptance. Section 10(2) adds a duty many people miss: each trustee accepting the trust must provide an affidavit that the trustee has read and understood the duties and liabilities of a trustee in Chapter IV of the Act. A trustee who disclaims must do so within sixty days under section 11, otherwise the trust property vests in him.

Step 2: Apply to the Assistant Director

Section 12 is the core rule of the new law: no trust shall be functional unless it is registered under this Act with the Assistant Director. The Assistant Director is the officer in each district under the Industries and Commerce Directorate of the Government of Sindh (section 2(a)). Under section 13, the trustee must provide the purpose of the trust, details of the author, the trustees, the beneficiaries, and any other natural person exercising ultimate effective control over the trust, as prescribed. Where there is more than one trustee, all trustees nominate one trustee to be responsible for providing this information.

Step 3: Verification of the Application

Under section 14, the Director verifies the contents and particulars of the application before registration through the investigating or prosecuting agencies. The original 2020 Act gave the agencies 14 days to submit their verified report, but the Sindh Trusts (Amendment) Act, 2021 replaced that fixed deadline with verification within such period and manner as may be prescribed. This verification is one of the main changes from the 1882 law, and it is why complete and accurate details matter at the application stage.

Step 4: Register the Property in the Trust's Name

Section 15 says all movable and immovable properties must be registered in the name of the trust under the Registration Act, 1908, and that registration must include the details of the author, the beneficiaries, the trustee, any co-trustee, and any other natural person exercising ultimate effective control over the trust. Registering the trust is not enough on its own: the property itself must stand in the trust's name.

Step 5: the Certificate of Registration

Under section 16, after the requirements of sections 13 to 15 are fulfilled, the Assistant Director registers the trust and issues a Certificate of Registration. Registration can be refused, for reasons recorded in writing, if the purpose is unlawful or the proceeds are suspected to be proceeds of crime, if any member is a proscribed person or associated with a proscribed organisation under the Anti-Terrorism Act, 1997 or the United Nations Security Council Act, 1948, if the District Intelligence Coordination Committee considers the trust a threat to national security, or if complete personal details are not provided.

Renewal Every Year

Registration is not a one-time job in Sindh. Under section 16-A, inserted by the Sindh Trusts (Amendment) Act, 2021, a trust must apply for renewal of its registration within the 30 days before expiry, except for specialised trusts, which follow a different procedure with a no objection certificate from the relevant regulator. Missing the renewal window can leave the trust unregistered and non-functional.

Penalties for Non-compliance

Section 17 gives the Assistant Director and the Director power to summon information and inspect the trust's record at any time. If a trustee violates the purpose of the trust or fails to provide information, the Director may impose a financial penalty of up to one million rupees, and failure to pay can lead to legal action resulting in imprisonment from one month to six months. Failing to provide information to the competent authorities can separately carry imprisonment of three months to six months.

Common Mistakes

First, signing a deed for immovable property and never registering it, which section 3 makes invalid. Second, registering the trust but never transferring the property into the trust's name, which sections 3 and 15 both require. Third, skipping the trustee affidavit under section 10(2). Fourth, giving incomplete details of beneficiaries or the person exercising ultimate effective control, which section 16 lists as a ground for refusal. Fifth, leaving a trust created under the old 1882 Act unregistered under the 2020 Act. Sixth, forgetting the annual renewal under section 16-A.

What This Means for You

Creating a trust in Sindh has five checkpoints: a clear deed, the trustee's acceptance affidavit, registration with the Assistant Director, the property registered in the trust's name, and the certificate of registration, followed by renewal every year. Check each one off yourself, even if someone files for you, because the liability for a missed step stays with the trustees.

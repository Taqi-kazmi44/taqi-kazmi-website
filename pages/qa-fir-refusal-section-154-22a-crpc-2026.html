Can the Police Refuse to Register Your FIR?

One of the most common complaints ordinary citizens make is that the police station refused to register their FIR. The law is clear on this point: where the information discloses a cognizable offence, registration is not a favour the police may grant or withhold. It is a duty, and the law provides two remedies if that duty is refused.

The legal rule: registration is mandatory (Section 154, CrPC 1898)

Section 154, titled "Information in cognizable cases", provides that every information relating to the commission of a cognizable offence, if given orally to an officer in charge of a police station, shall be reduced to writing by him or under his direction, read over to the informant, and signed by the person giving it, and the substance thereof shall be entered in a book kept for that purpose. A copy of the information as recorded must be given forthwith, free of cost, to the informant.

The word "every" is deliberate. The officer has no discretion to refuse registration because he doubts the truth or credibility of the information. Whether the information is true is a question for investigation, not a condition for registration.

First remedy: written complaint to the Superintendent of Police (Section 154(3))

Section 154(3) provides that any person aggrieved by a refusal on the part of an officer in charge of a police station to record the information may send the substance of such information, in writing and by post, to the Superintendent of Police concerned. If satisfied that the information discloses a cognizable offence, the Superintendent must either investigate the case himself or direct an investigation by a subordinate police officer.

Second remedy: petition before the Justice of the Peace (Section 22-A(6))

Section 22-A(6) empowers the ex-officio Justice of the Peace, who under Section 25 CrPC is the Sessions Judge (and Additional Sessions Judges), to direct the Station House Officer to register an FIR. The Supreme Court settled the scope of this power in PLD 2007 SC 539: the only jurisdiction exercisable by the ex-officio Justice of the Peace is to examine whether the information disclosed by the applicant did or did not constitute a cognizable offence, and if it did, to direct the concerned SHO to record an FIR, without going into the veracity of the information.

In practice, the petition is filed before the Sessions Judge or Additional Sessions Judge of the district, with a copy of the written complaint already given to the police. Courts expect the complainant to have first approached the police and, in practice, the Superintendent of Police, before invoking Section 22-A.

An important limit: this applies to cognizable offences

Sections 154 and 22-A(6) apply where the information discloses a cognizable offence, that is, an offence for which the police may arrest without a warrant. For a non-cognizable offence, Section 155 CrPC applies instead: the information is entered in a separate register, and investigation requires an order of a Magistrate. A refusal to register an FIR is therefore only unlawful where the complaint, on its face, discloses a cognizable offence.

The key legal point

  1. What this means for you: if a police station refuses your FIR, keep a copy of your written complaint, send it to the Superintendent of Police under Section 154(3), and if that fails, file a Section 22-A petition before the Justice of the Peace. Do not give up after a verbal refusal, and do not pay anyone to get an FIR registered, since registration is free of cost.

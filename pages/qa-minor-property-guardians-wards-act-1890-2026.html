Can a Minor's Property Be Sold in Pakistan?

One of the most common questions families ask: a child owns a share in a house or land, and the guardian wants to sell it. Can the guardian sell it on their own? The answer is: not without the court's permission. The Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 protects a minor's property with a clear rule, and the courts have recently reaffirmed it.

What Section 29 of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 says

Section 29 is titled "Limitation of powers of guardian of property appointed or declared by the Court." It says that where a person, other than a Collector or a guardian appointed by will or other instrument, has been appointed or declared by the Court to be guardian of the property of a ward, he shall not, without the previous permission of the Court, mortgage or charge, or transfer by sale, gift, exchange or otherwise, any part of the immovable property of his ward, or lease any part of that property for a term exceeding five years or for any term extending more than one year beyond the date on which the ward will cease to be a minor.

In plain words: a court-appointed guardian of a minor's property cannot sell, mortgage or gift the child's house, plot or land, and cannot lease it for a long term, without first getting the court's permission.

Section 31: permission only for necessity or evident advantage

Permission is not automatic. Under Section 31, permission to do any of the acts mentioned in Section 29 shall not be granted by the Court except in case of necessity or for an evident advantage to the ward. The court may also impose conditions, such as sale by public auction or deposit of the sale price into court or a bank in the minor's name. Typical examples of necessity are the child's education, medical treatment or upkeep, and an evident advantage could be an offer well above market value.

Section 30: a sale without permission is voidable

Under Section 30, a disposal of immovable property by a guardian in contravention of Section 28 or Section 29 is voidable at the instance of any other person affected thereby. That means the child, or someone acting for the child, can later ask a court to cancel the sale. The Federal Constitutional Court reaffirmed this protection in July 2026 in C.P.L.A. 30-L/2025, holding that the court acts as parens patriae and must jealously safeguard a minor's rights, and that permission under Section 29 is not an empty formality but a substantive safeguard.

What this means for you

If a minor owns a share in a house or land, no guardian can sell that share on their own. The sale needs the Guardian Court's permission first, and the court will only allow it if the sale is necessary or clearly good for the child. Before signing any deal involving a minor's property, check whether court permission was obtained, because a sale made without it can be challenged and set aside.

The key legal point

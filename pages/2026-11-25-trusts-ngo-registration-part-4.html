Trusts and NGO Registration (4/6): The Section 42 Company, a Not-for-Profit Company

A trust works for a family or a small charity. A society works for a membership body. But when an organisation expects large donations, institutional donors, or work across several provinces, it often needs something stronger: a company that cannot pay dividends, with a board, audited accounts and a licence from the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan. That is the Section 42 company under the Companies Act, 2017. This part explains what it is, how the licence works, and how it can be lost.

What a Section 42 Company is

Section 42(1) of the Companies Act, 2017 says that where it is proved to the satisfaction of the Commission that an association is to be formed as a limited company for promoting commerce, art, science, religion, health, education, research, sports, protection of environment, social welfare, charity or any other useful object, and that the company intends to apply its profits and other income in promoting its objects, and prohibits the payment of dividends to its members, and that its objects and activities are not and shall not, at any time, be against the laws, public order, security, sovereignty and national interests of Pakistan, the Commission may, by licence for a period to be specified, permit the association to be registered as a public limited company, without addition of the word "Limited" or the expression "(Guarantee) Limited" to its name. Three tests must all be met: a useful object, no dividends, and nothing against the laws, public order, security, sovereignty or national interests of Pakistan. Section 42(6) makes the licence compulsory: no association shall be registered as a company with these objects and conditions without a licence granted under this section.

The Licence and Its Conditions

Section 42(2) says a licence may be granted on such conditions and subject to such regulations as the Commission thinks fit, and those conditions are inserted in and deemed part of the memorandum and articles, or in one of those documents. The licence conditions are therefore not side terms. They become part of the company's own constitution, and breaking them is breaking the memorandum itself. Section 42(3) says the memorandum and articles of a company licensed under this section must be in accordance with the form set out in Table F in the First Schedule, or as near thereto as circumstances admit, and approved by the Commission. The licence is applied for from SECP, and the company is then registered as a public limited company. Section 42(4) says the association on registration enjoys all the privileges and is subject to all the obligations of a limited company. It is a full company in law, with the same standing as any other limited company, but it cannot pay dividends to its members.

Why Organisations Choose It

A Section 42 company suits a larger organisation for three reasons. First, it has the legal strength of a company: a board of directors, a memorandum and articles, and the full compliance framework of the Companies Act, 2017, including annual general meetings, annual returns and audited financial statements. Second, the SECP licence itself is a mark of credibility for donors, because the Commission checks the objects and the conditions before granting it. Third, it works across provinces, because it is a company under a federal law, not a society or trust registered in one province. That is why organisations expecting international grants, large corporate donations or work across several provinces often choose this route over a society or a trust. The price of that strength is heavier compliance than a society or a trust.

Losing the Licence

Section 42(5) gives the Commission power to revoke a licence at any time by order in writing, with such directions as it may deem fit, on being satisfied of any of these grounds: the company or its management has failed to comply with any term or condition of the licence; any requirement of section 42(1) or any regulation made under the section is not met; the affairs of the company are conducted in a manner prejudicial to public interest; the company has defaulted in filing its financial statements or annual returns for the immediately preceding two consecutive financial years; the company has acted against the interest, sovereignty and integrity of Pakistan, the security of the State and friendly relations with foreign States; the number of members is reduced below three; the company is conceived or brought forth for, or is or has been carrying on, unlawful or fraudulent activities; it is run and managed by persons who fail to maintain proper and true accounts, or commit fraud, misfeasance or malfeasance; it is run by persons involved in terrorist financing or money laundering; it is managed by persons who refuse to act according to the memorandum or articles or the Act, or fail to carry out directions of the Commission or the registrar; it is not carrying on its business or is not in operation for one year; or it is just and equitable that the licence should be revoked. Before revocation, the Commission must give the company notice in writing of its intention to do so and afford the company an opportunity to be heard. The two-year filing default is the ground that catches the most organisations, because a charity that stops filing accounts can lose its licence even if its work is honest.

What Happens After Revocation

Section 43 sets out the consequences of revocation, and they are severe. On revocation, the company must stop all its activities except the recovery of money owed to it, and it must not solicit or receive donations from any source. All the assets of the company, after satisfaction of all debts and liabilities, must be transferred to another company licensed under section 42, preferably having similar or identical objects, within ninety days from the revocation, or such extended period as the Commission may allow. A reasonable amount to meet the expenses of voluntary winding up or striking the name off the register may be retained. After compliance, the board must file a report to the registrar within fifteen days. Within thirty days of acceptance of that report, the board must initiate voluntary winding up, or where there are no assets and liabilities, apply to the registrar for striking the name of the company off the register. If the company fails to comply within the period specified, the Commission may appoint an administrator. As with a society, the assets never go to the members.

Common Mistakes

First, registering a company with charitable objects without a Section 42 licence, when section 42(6) forbids it. Second, treating the licence conditions as formalities, when section 42(2) makes them part of the memorandum and articles. Third, paying out profits or benefits to members in another form, when the whole point of section 42(1)(b) is that profits are applied to the objects and dividends are prohibited. Fourth, letting the number of members fall below three, which is itself a ground for revocation under section 42(5)(f). Fifth, missing financial statement and annual return filings for two consecutive years, which is a ground for revocation under section 42(5)(d). Sixth, assuming the licence is permanent, when it is granted for a period specified in the licence and can be revoked after notice and hearing.

What This Means for You

Before you choose this route, ask three questions. Is your object a useful object under section 42(1)(a)? Are you ready to apply every rupee of profit to that object and never pay a dividend? And can you keep up with company compliance, including audited accounts and annual filings, every year without fail? If the answer to all three is yes, a Section 42 company gives your organisation the strongest legal form available for not-for-profit work in Pakistan.

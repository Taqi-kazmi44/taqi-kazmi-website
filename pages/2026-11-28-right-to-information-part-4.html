Right to Information: What to Do When Your Request Is Refused

A refusal is not the end of the road. The Act gives you two remedies, and both have deadlines, so the clock matters more than the argument.

First, read the refusal. Section 8(5) says that where the Designated Official decides not to provide the information, he shall intimate the applicant the reasons for such decision, along with a statement that the applicant may file an internal review. A refusal without reasons is not a proper refusal. Silence also counts: if the officer does not reply within the 15 working days in section 8(3), or the extra 10 working days where the information is not readily available, that failure to communicate a decision within the specified time is itself a ground for internal review under section 9(1)(a). Keep the refusal letter, and keep your acknowledgement, because both prove the dates.

Remedy 1: internal review, section 9. Section 9(1) says that where the Designated Official decides not to provide the information, the applicant shall request the head of the public body for internal review of the decision. The grounds in section 9(1) are: (a) a failure by the Designated Official to comply with any provision of the Act, including failure to communicate a decision within the specified time; (b) unreasonable behaviour by the Designated Official in the exercise of any direction under the Act; (c) provision of incomplete, misleading or false information under the Act; and (d) any other matter relating to requesting or obtaining access to information.

Section 9(2) gives you 60 days from the date of communication of the decision, or from the failure to provide information within the stipulated time, to submit the request in writing and specify the remedy you seek against the decision of the Designated Official. Do not miss this window. Section 9(3) says the officer before whom the internal review application is filed may exercise any of the powers of the Designated Official under the Act and shall, within 30 days of receipt of the application: (a) confirm, modify or reverse the decision of the Designated Official; (b) notify the decision of internal review to the applicant, including reasons for the decision; and (c) order departmental action against the Designated Official if found negligent in performance of duties under the Act.

Remedy 2: complaint to the Commission, section 11. Section 11(1) says anyone who believes that his request has not been dealt with in accordance with the provisions of the Act has the right to lodge a complaint with the Commission to this effect. Section 11(2) says a complaint under section 11(1) shall be free of charge. Section 11(3) says the Commission shall decide any complaint within a period of 45 days. Section 11(4) places the burden of proof on the applicant to show that the Designated Official or public body acted in violation of the Act, so keep your application, acknowledgement, refusal, and internal review papers together: they are your proof.

Section 13 gives the Commission real teeth. Under section 13(1)(a) it may conduct an inquiry on a complaint and direct a public body to disclose information to the applicant. Under section 13(2) it shall decide a complaint within 45 days of its receipt and pass appropriate orders, including recommending disciplinary proceedings against delinquent officials. Under section 13(3) it may exercise the powers of a civil court to summon and enforce attendance of persons, compel oral or written evidence on oath, require production of documents or information, examine and inspect information, receive evidence on affidavits, requisition relevant information from any office, and issue summons for witnesses or documents. Under section 13(4) the Commission, or a person authorised by it, may examine any information on the spot while inquiring into a complaint.

Which remedy first? The Act does not say you must complete the internal review before complaining to the Commission. But the internal review is the step the refusal letter itself must point to under section 8(5), it is decided in 30 days, and it can end the matter inside the same office. If the head of the public body confirms the refusal, or does not decide within 30 days, the complaint to the Commission is your next step. Either way, act inside the 60-day window for internal review, and keep every paper from the start.

Common mistakes: first, accepting a verbal refusal, when section 8(5) requires written reasons and a statement about internal review. Second, missing the 60-day limit for internal review in section 9(2). Third, filing a complaint without the acknowledgement, the application copy, and the refusal, when section 11(4) puts the burden of proof on you. Fourth, going to court first, when the Act’s own remedies are free, have fixed deadlines of 30 and 45 days, and can direct disclosure and recommend disciplinary action.

What this means for you: a refusal must be in writing, with reasons, and it must tell you about internal review. You have 60 days to ask the head of the public body to review it, and the head must decide within 30 days. If that fails, the Sindh Information Commission must decide your free complaint within 45 days, and it can order the information disclosed.

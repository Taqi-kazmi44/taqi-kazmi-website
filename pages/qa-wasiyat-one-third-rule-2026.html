Can a Muslim Bequeath His Entire Property by Will in Pakistan?

One of the most common questions people ask: can a father leave his whole property to one child, or to anyone he likes, by will? The short answer is no. Under Hanafi Muslim law, a will (wasiyat) can dispose of only one-third of the estate. This page states the rule exactly as the Supreme Court and the High Court have stated it.

The one-third rule: 2025 SCMR 1673

The Supreme Court held in Ehsan-ul-Haq v. Muhammad Nawaz, 2025 SCMR 1673, that under Hanafi Muslim law a will may be verbal or in writing, but a Muslim cannot by will dispose of more than one-third of the surplus of his estate after payment of funeral expenses and debts. First the funeral expenses and the debts are paid. Of what is left, the will can cover one-third. The remaining two-thirds, at least, must go to the legal heirs in their fixed shares under Islamic inheritance law (faraid). A will cannot rewrite those fixed shares, and it cannot disinherit an heir.

A bequest above one-third needs the heirs' consent

A bequest beyond the legal third may be validated by the consent of the heirs. If they do not consent, the excess does not take effect and the remaining two-thirds must go to the heirs in the shares prescribed by law. Consent given by the heirs cannot be withdrawn once given.

A bequest to an heir also needs the other heirs' consent

A bequest in favour of a person who is already a legal heir is not valid unless the other heirs consent after the testator's death. The Sindh High Court applied this in Bahadur Golo v. Qaimuddin Golo, 2026 CLC 557, holding that a will deed in favour of one heir, without the other heirs' consent, was ineffective in law beyond one-third of the property. The same principle is stated in Mulla, Principles of Mahomedan Law, paragraph 117. So a father cannot leave his whole house to one son by will while the other children get nothing, unless all the other heirs agree after his death.

The Succession Act, 1925 does not govern Muslim wills

Section 58(1) of the Succession Act, 1925 provides that Part VI of the Act, which deals with testamentary succession, shall not apply to testamentary succession to the property of any Muhammadan. A Muslim's will is governed by Muslim personal law, not by the Succession Act. That is also why a Muslim will need not be in writing and need not be attested in the way the Succession Act requires for others, though a written will signed before two witnesses is the practical standard because it is far easier to prove.

One important note: this is the Hanafi position

The rule stated here is Hanafi law, which governs the overwhelming majority of Muslims in Pakistan. The Shia (Fiqh-e-Jafaria) position differs on one point: under Shia law, a bequest to an heir within the one-third is valid even without the other heirs' consent. Anyone who follows Fiqh-e-Jafaria should confirm the position for their own case.

The key legal point

  1. What this means for you: a will can direct up to one-third of your estate, after debts and funeral expenses, to a non-heir such as a charity or a relative who would not otherwise inherit. It cannot give your whole property to one person or change your heirs' fixed shares.
  2. Anything beyond one-third, or any bequest to an heir, works only if the other heirs consent after your death.

SC: Trial Courts Can Reject Time-Barred Suits on Their Own

Supreme Court (bench headed by Justice Muhammad Ali Mazhar, eight-page order): trial courts can reject a time-barred or palpably vexatious plaint on their own initiative at the earliest stage, without waiting for a defendant's application, under Section 3 of the Limitation Act 1908 and Order VII, Rule 11 CPC. A plaint cannot be rejected in parts. Verified (Dawn, 10 Oct 2026).

A judge no longer has to wait for anyone to ask before throwing out a time-barred or frivolous suit.
The Supreme Court says trial courts can do it on their own, at the very start.

In an eight-page order, a three-judge bench headed by Justice Muhammad Ali Mazhar ruled that nothing in Section 3 of the Limitation Act, 1908 or Order VII, Rule 11 of the Civil Procedure Code stops a court from rejecting a plaint on its own initiative, without any application from the defendant, if the suit is barred by law.

The case arose from a dispute over a bungalow in Askari-IV, Karachi. The trial court had rejected the plaint in September 2024 as time-barred. The Sindh High Court set that rejection aside, but the defendant, Muhammad Islam Badshah, appealed to the Supreme Court, which set aside the SHC's February 6, 2025 order and sent the case back for a fresh decision on maintainability after hearing both sides.

What the judgment lays down:
- A court can act even before issuing summons to the other side, saving judicial time and shielding parties from vexatious litigation.
- Before rejecting a plaint, the court must first check the Order VII, Rule 11 grounds: whether a cause of action is disclosed, whether the relief is properly valued and stamped, and whether the suit appears barred by law from the plaint's own statements.
- A plaint cannot be rejected in parts. If even one prayer is maintainable, the whole plaint survives.
- Where questions of law and fact are intertwined, the suit should proceed to the written statement and discovery stages.

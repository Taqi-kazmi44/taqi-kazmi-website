How to Get a Guardianship Certificate for a Minor: A Step-by-Step Guide

When a child's parents are gone or unable to care for them, a relative cannot simply step in on paper. Banks, schools and passport offices ask for proof. That proof is a guardianship certificate from the court. This guide walks you through the exact procedure under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890.

The step-by-step procedure

  1. Decide what kind of guardianship you need. Under Section 7 of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, the court can appoint a guardian of the child's person, of the child's property, or of both. A school admission usually needs guardianship of the person. Selling or managing the child's property needs guardianship of the property.
  2. File in the right court. Under Section 9, an application for guardianship of the person goes to the District Court where the minor ordinarily resides. For property, you may also file where the property is situated. Under Section 8, the applicant can be the person seeking guardianship, a relative or friend of the minor, or the District Collector.
  3. Prepare the petition carefully. Section 10 requires the petition to state the minor's name, sex, religion, date of birth and ordinary residence, the near relations of the minor and where they live, the nature and approximate value of any property, who currently has custody, and the qualifications of the proposed guardian. Attach the minor's birth certificate or B-Form, your CNIC, proof of relationship, and the parent's death certificate where applicable. The proposed guardian must also sign a declaration of willingness to act, attested by at least two witnesses (Section 10(3)).
  4. Expect notice to others. Under Section 11, the court fixes a hearing date and gives notice to the minor's parents, the person having custody, the proposed guardian, and any other person the court considers should be informed. Notice is also displayed publicly.
  5. Attend the hearing and prove your case. Under Section 13, the court hears evidence for and against the application. Keep your documents ready: proof of residence, income or means, and anything showing your relationship with the child and your ability to care for them.
  6. Remember the test the court applies. Under Section 17, the court is guided by the welfare of the minor, consistent with the law to which the minor is subject. It considers the child's age, sex and religion, your character and capacity, how closely you are related to the child, the wishes of a deceased parent, and your existing relationship with the child or their property. If the child is old enough to form an intelligent preference, the court may consider that preference too. No one can be made a guardian against their will (Section 17(5)).
  7. Get the order and use a certified copy. If satisfied, the court makes an order under Section 7 appointing or declaring you guardian. Obtain a certified copy of the order and present it wherever proof of guardianship is asked for, such as NADRA, a bank or a school. Note one limit: Section 19 bars the court from appointing a guardian for the person of a minor in some cases, for example where a living parent is not unfit to be guardian.

The governing law

The procedure is governed by the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 (Act No. VIII of 1890): Section 7 empowers the court to appoint or declare a guardian; Section 8 lists who may apply; Section 9 fixes jurisdiction; Section 10 prescribes the form of the application; Section 11 prescribes notice; Section 13 prescribes the hearing of evidence; Section 17 prescribes the welfare test; and Section 19 lists cases in which a guardian may not be appointed.

The key legal point

  1. What this means for you: a court order, not a family agreement, is what turns care of a child into legal authority that institutions will accept.

PLD 2026 SC 238: The Voice of the Child Must Be Heard in Custody Cases

A two-member bench of the Supreme Court of Pakistan, comprising Justice Syed Mansoor Ali Shah and Justice Aqeel Ahmed Abbasi, held in Dr Muhammad Asif vs Dr Sana Sattar and others, reported as PLD 2026 SC 238 (C.R.P. No. 458 of 2024 in C.P.L.A. No. 2514 of 2024, judgment authored by Justice Shah, decided May 2025), that hearing and respecting the voice of the child in custody and guardianship cases is a binding obligation under the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, not an aspirational goal. The best interests of the child are the paramount consideration.

What was decided

  1. Dr Sana Sattar (mother) sought custody of her two children before the senior civil judge (family division), Layyah. Her application was dismissed on 14 July 2023, though a visitation schedule was framed. Both parents appealed; the additional district judge, Layyah, granted custody to the mother and ordered a visitation arrangement for the father, Dr Muhammad Asif. The father's constitutional petition before the Multan bench of the Lahore High Court was dismissed on 24 May 2024, and his appeal to the Supreme Court met the same fate on 18 July. He then moved a review petition before the apex court.
  2. Dismissing the review, the Court held that courts, particularly family courts and judges of the district judiciary, must hear and respect the voice of the child in custody and guardianship cases. Justice Shah wrote that this is not an aspirational goal but a binding obligation under the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child. A child of sufficient age and maturity must be given a meaningful opportunity to express views, and those views must receive due consideration.
  3. The Court held that the best interests of the children are best served by keeping them in the custody of their mother, and directed both parties to strictly adhere to the visitation schedule determined by the lower appellate court. Custody continues with the mother unless and until the father moves a fresh application before a competent forum on a material change in circumstances. The judgment calls on courts to adopt a child-friendly, participatory and child-justice approach in every case involving children.
  4. The judgment has since been followed: the Sindh High Court has relied on it in custody disputes, and the SHC Legal Research Centre has recorded its shift from the traditional welfare doctrine to a broader rights-of-the-child approach, reading Section 17 of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 in harmony with Articles 3 and 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child.

The key legal point

  1. For ordinary people: if custody of your children is being decided, the court is required to hear your child as well, not just the parents. Your child is not a passive subject of the proceedings but a rights-bearing individual whose dignity must be safeguarded at every stage.
  2. For law students, this case is essential reading on interpreting Section 17 of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 in harmony with Articles 3 and 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, on the shift from welfare of the child to the rights of the child, and on the limits of writ jurisdiction in family matters.
  3. Practical note: the Court's visitation framework keeps both parents meaningfully involved. Welfare goes far beyond financial or physical well-being: emotional, psychological, educational, cultural and developmental needs all count in a custody decision.

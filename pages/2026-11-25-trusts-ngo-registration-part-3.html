Trusts and NGO Registration (3/6): Registering a Society under the Act of 1860

Your neighbourhood wants a library, your old school friends want an alumni association, or your community wants a welfare organisation that collects donations and runs a clinic. The oldest and simplest legal form for that is a society. The law that governs it is 166 years old and still in force: the Societies Registration Act, 1860. This part explains who can form a society, what papers are needed, and what rights and duties follow registration.

What a Society is

Section 1 of the Act says any seven or more persons associated for any literary, scientific or charitable purpose, or for any purpose described in section 20, may form themselves into a society by subscribing their names to a memorandum of association and filing it with the Registrar of Joint-stock Companies. Section 20 lists the purposes the Act covers: charitable societies, societies for the promotion of science, literature or the fine arts, for instruction, the diffusion of useful knowledge, the diffusion of political education, the foundation or maintenance of libraries or reading rooms for general use among the members or open to the public, or public museums and galleries of painting and other works of art, collections of natural history, mechanical and philosophical inventions, instruments or designs. A society is a membership body. It is not a company, and it does not distribute profit. Any income must be used for the objects of the society, and on dissolution the assets go to another society, not to the members.

The Memorandum of Association

Section 2 says the memorandum of association must contain three things: the name of the society, the objects of the society, and the names, addresses and occupations of the governors, council, directors, committee or other governing body to whom, by the rules of the society, the management of its affairs is entrusted. A copy of the rules and regulations of the society, certified to be a correct copy by not less than three of the members of the governing body, must be filed with the memorandum. Write the objects carefully, because everything the society does afterwards must fit inside them. A vague object causes trouble later, and changing the objects needs a special procedure under section 12.

Registration and the Certificate

Section 3 says that upon the memorandum and certified copy being filed, the Registrar shall certify under his hand that the society is registered under the Act. The fee for registration is such fee as the Provincial Government may direct, so it is fixed by the province, not by the Act. In Sindh, the file goes to the Registrar of Societies in the Industries and Commerce Department, Government of Sindh. The government fee is around Rs 10,500 at present in Sindh, but confirm the current fee at filing, because the Act leaves it to the Provincial Government. Once the certificate is issued, the society exists as a registered body.

Property and Suits

Section 5 says the property of a registered society, movable and immovable, if not vested in trustees, is deemed to be vested for the time being in the governing body of the society. Section 6 says a registered society may sue or be sued in the name of the president, chairman, or principal secretary, or trustees, as determined by the rules of the society, and if the rules say nothing, in the name of such person as the governing body appoints for the occasion. Section 7 says no suit or proceeding in a Civil Court abates or discontinues because the person who sued or was sued in the society's name dies or ceases to hold that character. The suit continues in the name of or against the successor. Section 8 protects the office bearers: if a judgment is recovered against the person or officer named on behalf of the society, the judgment is enforced against the property of the society, not against the property or the body of that person or officer.

Members: Rights and Liabilities

Section 15 defines a member as a person who, having been admitted according to the rules, has paid a subscription or signed the roll or list of members, and has not resigned in accordance with the rules. A member whose subscription is in arrear for more than three months cannot vote or be counted as a member. Section 9 says a pecuniary penalty imposed by a bye-law duly made under the rules can be recovered in court. Section 10 says a member in arrear of subscription, or who detains, injures or destroys society property, may be sued for the arrear or the damage in the same manner as a stranger. Section 11 goes further: a member who steals, purloins or embezzles money or property of the society, wilfully destroys or injures its property, or forges a deed, bond, security, receipt or other instrument exposing the society's funds to loss, is subject to the same prosecution and punishment as any person who is not a member. Membership gives no protection from the law.

Changing the Objects

Section 12 lets a society alter, extend or abridge its purposes, or amalgamate with another society, but only through a strict procedure. The governing body submits the proposition in a written or printed report and convenes a special meeting. The report must be delivered or sent by post to every member ten days before the special meeting. The proposition must be agreed to by the votes of three-fifths of the members delivered in person or by proxy, and confirmed by the votes of three-fifths of the members present at a second special meeting convened at an interval of one month after the first meeting. One meeting is not enough. The law deliberately makes changing the objects slow, so that a small group cannot hijack a society's purpose.

Dissolution: the Money Never Goes to Members

Section 13 says any number not less than three-fifths of the members may determine that the society shall be dissolved, and it is then dissolved forthwith or at the time agreed upon. The property, claims and liabilities are settled according to the rules of the society, or if there are none, as the governing body finds expedient. If a dispute arises, the adjustment of affairs is referred to the principal Court of original civil jurisdiction of the district in which the chief building of the society is situate. If the Government is a member of or a contributor to, or otherwise interested in, the society, it cannot be dissolved without the consent of the Government of the Province of registration. Section 14 is the most important rule in the Act for donors: if upon dissolution any property remains after satisfying all debts and liabilities, it shall not be paid to or distributed among the members, but shall be given to some other society, to be determined by the votes of not less than three-fifths of the members present personally or by proxy at the time of dissolution, or in default by the Court. This clause does not apply to a society founded by contributions of shareholders in the nature of a joint-stock company.

Supersession of the Governing Body

Section 16-A, added by later amendment, gives the Provincial Government a strong power. If after inquiry the Government is of the opinion that the governing body is unable to discharge or persistently fails in discharging its duties, is unable to administer its affairs or meet its financial obligations, or generally acts in a manner contrary to public interest or the interests of the members, it may by notification in the Official Gazette declare the governing body superseded for a period not exceeding one year. The declaration cannot be made without giving the society an opportunity of being heard. During supersession, the office bearers cease to hold office and a governing body constituted by the Provincial Government from among the members performs the functions. On expiry of the period, the governing body is reconstituted under the memorandum and rules.

The Annual Duty

Section 4 imposes a yearly filing. Once in every year, on or before the fourteenth day succeeding the day on which, according to the rules of the society, the annual general meeting is held, or if the rules do not provide for an annual general meeting, in the month of January, a list must be filed with the Registrar of the names, addresses and occupations of the governing body then entrusted with the management of the society. Many registered societies forget this filing for years. It is a simple duty, and keeping it up to date is the easiest way to keep the society's record clean.

Deeni Madaris

Section 21 was inserted by the Societies Registration (Amendment) Act, 2005 and substituted by the Amendment Act of 2006, and substituted again by the Societies Registration (Amendment) Act, 2024, which received the President's assent on 27 December 2024 and came into force at once. Under the current section 21, all Deeni Madaris, by whatsoever name called, shall not operate without getting themselves registered: a madrassah in existence before the 2024 Act, if not already registered, must register within six months of its commencement, and a madrassah established after its commencement must register within one year of its establishment. One madrassah with more than one campus needs only one registration. Every Deeni Madrassah must submit an annual report of its educational activities to the Registrar, and must have its accounts audited by an auditor and submit a copy of the audit report. No Deeni Madrassah may teach or publish literature which promotes militancy or spreads sectarianism or religious hatred, though comparative study of religions and schools of thought is expressly not barred. Every Deeni Madrassah must also, subject to its resources, include basic contemporary subjects in its curriculum under a phased programme. Once registered under the Act, a madrassah is not required to register under any other law. The 2024 amendment is a federal law for Islamabad, because education is a provincial subject after the 18th Amendment and the provinces legislate separately on this. A companion Ordinance added section 21-C, giving seminaries in Islamabad the choice of registering with the Directorate General of Religious Education or with the deputy commissioner's office.

Common Mistakes

First, starting with fewer than seven persons, when section 1 requires seven or more. Second, filing a memorandum without the objects written clearly, or without the rules certified by at least three members of the governing body, as section 2 requires. Third, treating the society as the personal property of its founders, when section 14 forbids any distribution of assets to members on dissolution. Fourth, ignoring the annual list under section 4, which leaves the society's record out of date. Fifth, changing the objects by a single meeting, when section 12 requires two meetings a month apart with three-fifths majorities. Sixth, assuming the society can do anything it likes with donations, when the objects in the memorandum and the rules are the legal limit of its work.

What This Means for You

Before you register a society, fix four things: at least seven members, a clear name and lawful objects, a named governing body with names, addresses and occupations, and rules certified by at least three members of the governing body. Then file with the Registrar, keep the annual list current, and remember that the society's property belongs to the society and its objects, never to the members.

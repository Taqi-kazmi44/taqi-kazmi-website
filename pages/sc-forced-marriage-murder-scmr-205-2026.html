2026 SCMR 205: Refusal of Marriage Is a Constitutional Right

A three-member bench of the Supreme Court of Pakistan, comprising Justice Muhammad Hashim Khan Kakar, Justice Salahuddin Panhwar and Justice Ishtiaq Ibrahim, held in Muhammad Kamran vs The State and Another, reported as 2026 SCMR 205 and decided on 16 May 2025 (Criminal Appeal No. 91 of 2024), that no one has the right to force another into marriage, and that taking a life over a refusal is an affront to the individual, to justice, and to the autonomy and dignity of women. The Court affirmed the death sentence.

What was decided

  1. Muhammad Kamran proposed marriage to his cousin, Mst. Maimuna Shaheen. When she refused, he entered her house on the morning of 27 January 2011 and shot her in the chest, ending her life. An FIR was registered the same day, the trial court sentenced him to death with a fine, and the High Court upheld the verdict.
  2. The Supreme Court affirmed the conviction and the sentence, and laid down the governing rule: no one has the right to force another into marriage. The autonomy and dignity of women are protected by law, and taking a life because of a refusal to marry is an affront not only to the individual but to justice and to women's autonomy.
  3. Invoking Article 9 of the Constitution, the Court held that a woman's consent is a legal and constitutional right. It warned that there can be no leniency in cases of gender-based violence, and that any soft stance would set a dangerous and misleading judicial precedent.
  4. The Court added that it is a disgraceful act when a man, unable to accept a woman's refusal, resorts to tormenting or harming her. Wounded ego and notions of honour are not defences known to the law in such cases.

The key legal point

  1. For ordinary people, the message is empowering: refusing a marriage proposal is your right, and the law stands with the person who says no. Violence over a refusal is not an honour issue, it is a crime with no room for leniency.
  2. For law students, this case is essential reading on the application of Article 9 to gender-based violence, on reading motive and intent through the vital-organ rule, and on how rights-sensitive judicial language shapes precedent.
  3. Practical note: the judgment does not lower the evidentiary bar for conviction, but it firmly shuts the door on wounded-ego or honour defences in killings committed over a refused proposal.

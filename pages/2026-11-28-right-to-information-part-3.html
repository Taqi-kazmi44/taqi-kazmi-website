Right to Information: The Fifteen Categories Every Office Must Publish

Some information should never need an application. The law says fifteen categories of government information must already be public before any citizen asks.

The rule: section 6. Section 6(1) says that, subject to the provisions of the Act, a public body shall proactively disclose and publish fifteen categories of information. This is a duty, not a favour. It applies to every public body covered by the Act, from a provincial department to a local government office. Section 6(2) requires every public body to disseminate all this information as widely as possible, including on the internet where possible, so that all citizens have easy access to it. Section 6(3) adds that every public body shall endeavour to ensure proactive disclosure of information as explained in section 6(1). Read with section 5, which requires every public body to maintain its records properly so it can comply with the Act, the message is clear: publication is part of the job, not an extra.

The fifteen categories in section 6(1) are:

  1. (a) Particulars of its organisation, functions and duties.
  2. (b) Powers and functions of its officers and employees.
  3. (c) Norms and criteria set by it for the discharge of its daily functions.
  4. (d) Rules, regulations, notifications, circulars, instructions and other legal instruments being enforced, issued or used by it or being used by its employees in the discharge of its functions.
  5. (e) A statement of the categories of information that are under its control.
  6. (f) A description of its decision making processes.
  7. (g) Details of all its administrative and developmental decisions.
  8. (h) A directory of its officers and employees with their qualifications and respective remuneration, perks and privileges.
  9. (i) Its budget, including details of all proposed and actual expenditures.
  10. (j) The manner of execution of subsidy programmes.
  11. (k) Full particulars of the recipients of concessions, permits or authorisations granted by it.
  12. (l) Facilities available with the public body for obtaining information held by it.
  13. (m) Complete particulars of its public information officer.
  14. (n) Any other information as may be prescribed.
  15. (o) Maintenance of record in respect of applications received and actions taken on them.

Why these fifteen matter: four of these categories are the ones ordinary citizens use most. The budget and actual expenditure under (i) show where public money was planned to go and where it actually went. The officers directory under (h) shows who holds which post, with what qualifications, and on what pay, perks and privileges. The decisions under (g) show what the office actually decided, not what it announced. And the recipients of concessions, permits and authorisations under (k) show who received a benefit from the office. Before you file a request under section 8, check whether the answer is already published under section 6. If it is not published, that itself is a failure of the duty in section 6, and you can say so in your application.

Common mistakes: first, assuming every piece of information needs a written application, when section 6 requires these fifteen categories to be published without anyone asking. Second, accepting “it is not on our website” as a final answer, when section 6(2) requires the widest possible dissemination, including the internet where possible. Third, asking for a rule or notification that the office was already bound to publish under (d), instead of first checking its notice board or website. Fourth, overlooking the record of applications received and actions taken under (o), which shows how the office has handled other citizens’ requests.

What this means for you: before writing a request, look for the budget, the officers directory, the rules and the decisions the office was supposed to publish on its own. If they are published, you have your answer without an application. If they are not, you now know the office is not meeting section 6, and your request can point that out.

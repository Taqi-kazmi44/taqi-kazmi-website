Trusts and NGO Registration (1/6): What Is a Trust, and What Changed in 2020

You want to set aside property for your children, or run a charity for education or healthcare. A trust is one of the oldest legal tools for exactly that. But in Sindh, the law that governs trusts changed completely in 2020, and most people still quote the old 1882 law. This six-part series explains trusts and NGO registration in plain language, starting with the basics: what a trust is, who is involved, and what the new law says.

What a Trust is

Section 2(v) of the Sindh Trusts Act, 2020 defines a trust as an obligation annexed to the ownership of property, arising out of confidence reposed in and accepted by the owner, or declared and accepted by him, for the benefit of a beneficiary. In simple words, one person hands over property to another person to hold and manage it for the benefit of someone else. Four roles matter. The author of the trust is the person who creates it (section 2(b)). The trustee is the person who accepts the confidence and manages the property (section 2(x)). The beneficiary is the person who benefits (section 2(c)). The trust property is the subject matter of the trust, and it may be movable or immovable property (section 2(w)).

The Purpose Must Be Lawful

Section 2(q) says the purpose of a trust must be lawful. A purpose is unlawful if it is forbidden by law, defeats the provisions of any law, is fraudulent, involves injury to the person or property of another, or the court regards it as immoral or opposed to public policy. Section 4(2) is blunt: every trust for an unlawful purpose is void, and where a trust is created for more than one purpose and one of them is unlawful, the whole trust is void. Decide the purpose first, and write it clearly, because everything else in the trust depends on it.

Who Can Create a Trust

Section 5 says a trust may be created by every person competent to contract under the Contract Act, 1872, or, with the permission of a principal Civil Court of original jurisdiction, by or on behalf of a minor. The word natural was removed from this section by the Sindh Trusts (Amendment) Act, 2021, so the rule now reads every person, not every natural person. Competence to contract is the same test as in contract law: age of majority, sound mind, and no disqualification by law. A trust for a minor needs the court's permission first.

Who Can Be a Beneficiary or a Trustee

Section 7 says any person capable of holding property may be a beneficiary. Section 9 says any person capable of holding property may be a trustee, but where the trust involves the exercise of discretion, the trustee must be competent to contract. This is the law as amended: the original 2020 Act said every natural person and added an Explanation that a legal person cannot be a beneficiary or a trustee, but the Sindh Trusts (Amendment) Act, 2021 removed the word natural from both sections and omitted both Explanations. So a company or an organisation can now be a trustee or a beneficiary under the Sindh Act, which matters for specialised trusts such as funds and pension schemes. Only a person capable of holding property can hold these roles.

What Changed in 2020

For 138 years, trusts in Pakistan were governed by the Trusts Act, 1882. In 2020, that Act was repealed and replaced by five separate laws: one for each province, Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan, and one for the Islamabad Capital Territory. Sindh passed the Sindh Trusts Act, 2020 (Sindh Act No. XXIX of 2020), passed by the Provincial Assembly on 21 August 2020, assented to by the Governor on 22 September 2020, and notified on 28 September 2020. The preamble says the 1882 Act did not cater for the monitoring and evaluation of trusts, and the new laws were made after the 18th Constitutional Amendment, in response to Financial Action Task Force recommendations on money laundering and terrorist financing risks. The old concepts survive, but registration, disclosure and government oversight are now central. Trusts created under the old 1882 Act must also be re-registered under the new law to remain functional.

What This Act Does Not Cover

Section 1(4) says nothing in the Act affects the rules of Muslim law as to waqf, or the mutual relations of members of an undivided family as determined by customary or personal law, or applies to public or private religious or charitable endowments. A waqf is a different institution under Muslim law, and this series does not cover it.

Common Mistakes

First, quoting the Trusts Act, 1882 for a new trust in Sindh, when the Sindh Trusts Act, 2020 is the law in force. Second, quoting the original 2020 text of sections 7 and 9 without the 2021 amendment, which removed the natural person restriction and the Explanations that barred legal persons. Third, creating a trust for a vague or unlawful purpose, which section 4(2) makes void. Fourth, treating an unregistered trust as functional, when section 12 says no trust shall be functional unless it is registered with the Assistant Director. Fifth, confusing a trust with a waqf, which section 1(4) keeps outside this Act.

What This Means for You

Before you create a trust, fix four things: a lawful purpose, a named beneficiary, identified trust property, and a trustee who is a person capable of holding property, competent to contract where the trust involves discretion. Then register it under the 2020 Act as amended in 2021, because an unregistered trust does not function in law. Part 2 explains the trust deed and the registration process in Sindh, step by step.
